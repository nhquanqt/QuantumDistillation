# Literature Review: Quantum Machine Learning Distillation

Quantum machine learning (QML) studies how quantum computation can be used to improve or reformulate machine learning algorithms, often by exploiting quantum state spaces, superposition, entanglement, and measurement-based computation. Early QML research emphasized the possibility of quantum speedups for linear algebra, kernel methods, classification, and optimization, while also recognizing that practical deployment remains constrained by limited qubit counts, noise, and hardware scalability.

Knowledge distillation (KD) offers a natural solution to these constraints. In classical machine learning, KD transfers knowledge from a high-capacity teacher model to a smaller student model by training the student to match the teacher's output distribution or intermediate representations. In QML, distillation can be adapted in several ways: a classical teacher can supervise a quantum student, a larger QNN can supervise a smaller QNN, or quantum computation can be used to enhance the distillation process between classical models. The central motivation is to exploit the representational or computational properties of quantum models while mitigating the cost, instability, and noise sensitivity of training large quantum circuits directly.

## Biamonte et al. (2017)

Biamonte et al. describe QML as a field aimed at designing quantum software that may enable faster machine learning, but they also emphasize that both hardware and software challenges remain substantial. Their review spans quantum linear algebra, quantum optimization, quantum classifiers, and kernel-style learning methods, presenting QML as a broad research program rather than a single model family. This paper helps frame why QML distillation matters: even if quantum learning models are expressive, practical deployment remains limited by current hardware and algorithmic maturity. It therefore provides the high-level motivation for techniques that reduce circuit size, training cost, and hardware sensitivity. <sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

## Cerezo et al. (2020)

Within the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era, variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) and parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) have become central architectures for QML. These models typically combine a quantum circuit with trainable parameters and a classical optimization loop, making them suitable for near-term devices with limited depth and imperfect gates. Cerezo et al. identify VQAs as one of the leading strategies for using near-term quantum devices, while also highlighting persistent challenges in trainability, accuracy, and efficiency. Their review also surveys issues such as ansatz design, optimizer choice, noise robustness, and resource overhead, all of which directly affect whether a QML model can be trained and deployed effectively. This provides the broader optimization and deployment context in which QML distillation becomes useful: distillation can be seen as one practical route for simplifying VQAs after or during training. <sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>

## Schuld et al. (2020)

In supervised learning, circuit-centric quantum classifiers use low-depth parameterized circuits to encode inputs and produce class predictions through quantum measurements, showing that quantum models can be trained similarly to neural networks while using substantially fewer trainable parameters in some settings. Schuld et al. provide an important example of how shallow variational circuits can already function as practical classifiers, especially in small-data or proof-of-concept settings. This work is useful background because it shows the type of compact QML models that later distillation methods often target as students. It also suggests why distillation is appealing: if a small circuit can already express useful decision boundaries, teacher supervision may help it reach good solutions more reliably than direct end-to-end training alone. <sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>

## McClean et al. (2018)

Despite their promise, quantum neural networks (QNNs) face several practical limitations. One major issue is trainability: as circuit size grows, gradients can vanish exponentially, producing barren plateaus that make optimization extremely difficult. McClean et al. showed that for broad classes of parameterized quantum circuits, the probability of obtaining a useful gradient can become exponentially small as the number of qubits increases. This result is foundational because it shifts the challenge from raw expressivity to trainability: a model may be powerful in principle but unusable in practice if optimization signals disappear. In addition, deeper quantum circuits are more vulnerable to noise, decoherence, gate errors, and device-specific compilation overhead. These constraints motivate model compression and transfer learning strategies that can produce smaller, shallower, and more hardware-compatible quantum models without requiring full retraining from scratch. Distillation is therefore relevant not only as a compression tool, but also as a possible way to reduce optimization difficulty by transferring knowledge from an already trained model into a simpler circuit. <sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>

## Alam, Kundu, and Ghosh (2022)

Early work on QNN distillation focused on compressing quantum circuits themselves. Alam, Kundu, and Ghosh introduced knowledge distillation for QNNs using approximate synthesis, where a trained QNN is transformed into a shallower or hardware-adapted circuit with fewer layers or a different gate set. Rather than treating the student as an entirely new architecture, their approach is closely tied to circuit synthesis and device constraints, which makes it especially relevant for practical quantum compilation pipelines. Their method targets a key deployment problem: a circuit that performs well in noiseless simulation may perform poorly on real quantum hardware due to depth-dependent noise and device-specific gate constraints. Their experiments report a substantial reduction in circuit layers while improving noisy-hardware performance, suggesting that distillation can serve not only as model compression but also as a hardware-adaptation mechanism for QNNs. A key contribution of this paper is to show that in QML, "compression" may mean not just fewer parameters, but better compatibility with the target hardware's native gate set and noise profile. <sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>

## Hasan and Mahdy (2023)

A second line of work studies classical-to-quantum knowledge transfer. Hasan and Mahdy proposed a hybrid distillation framework in which classical convolutional neural networks such as LeNet and AlexNet act as teachers, while smaller variational quantum circuits serve as students. The quantum students are trained using supervisory signals from the classical teachers, including output-distribution matching through KL divergence. This is conceptually important because it removes the need to first train a large and expensive quantum teacher, instead leveraging the maturity of classical deep learning as a source of supervision. Their results on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST show that distillation can improve quantum student accuracy, especially on the more complex Fashion-MNIST dataset. This direction is important because it allows QML systems to reuse strong classical pretrained models rather than relying entirely on expensive or unstable quantum training. At the same time, it raises an interpretability question: improvements may come from better supervision and regularization, even when no uniquely quantum advantage is demonstrated. <sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>

## QD-LLM (2025)

More recent work extends QML distillation beyond small image classification. QD-LLM proposes quantum knowledge distillation for large language models, using variational quantum circuits as compact student models for text classification. The paper argues that QNN-based students had previously been explored mostly in small-scale image classification, while LLM distillation introduces a more challenging language-domain setting. This is a notable shift because it asks whether quantum students can participate in modern foundation-model compression workflows rather than only in toy benchmark settings. In simulation, QD-LLM reports competitive accuracy and efficiency with a small number of qubits, positioning quantum student models as a possible parameter-efficient alternative for distilling large classical models. Its main significance is less about proving immediate hardware utility and more about broadening the scope of QML distillation to language tasks and ultra-compact student architectures. The main limitation is that the evidence is still simulation-based and should be compared carefully against strong classical compression baselines. <sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>

## Quantum Relational Knowledge Distillation (2025)

Another emerging direction is quantum-enhanced distillation where the deployed teacher and student remain classical, but quantum computation is introduced during training. Quantum Relational Knowledge Distillation (QRKD) extends relational knowledge distillation by mapping classical features into Hilbert space and computing quantum kernel values to capture inter-sample relationships. Unlike output-logit matching, relational distillation focuses on preserving geometric structure between samples, so the quantum component here is used to enrich the similarity signal rather than to replace the final model. Unlike QNN-student approaches, QRKD maintains a fully classical inference pipeline, requiring quantum computation only during training. This design is attractive because it separates quantum-enhanced supervision from deployment constraints, allowing standard classical models to benefit from quantum relational structure without requiring quantum hardware at inference time. Conceptually, this makes QRKD one of the clearest examples of quantum resources being used as a training-time regularizer instead of as the deployed predictor itself. <sup><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup>

## QuantumMedKD (2025)

Application-specific variants are also beginning to appear. QuantumMedKD proposes a hybrid quantum-classical knowledge distillation framework for medical image analysis, using classical CNN teachers and parameter-efficient quantum student networks for pediatric chest X-ray pneumonia detection. This work is notable because it moves QML distillation into a domain where compact inference and deployment efficiency can matter in real operational settings. The study reports that quantum student models can use very few trainable parameters compared with classical CNNs while maintaining competitive diagnostic performance, indicating that QML distillation may be useful in resource-constrained or edge-like healthcare scenarios. At the same time, its domain-specific framing also highlights a recurring challenge for the literature: promising application results still need broader validation across datasets, hardware conditions, and stronger classical baselines before general conclusions can be drawn. <sup><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup>

## Cross-Paper Synthesis

Overall, the literature suggests that quantum machine learning distillation is still an early but rapidly developing research area. Existing studies can be grouped into three main categories: quantum-to-quantum distillation, where a large or deep QNN is compressed into a smaller or more hardware-compatible QNN; classical-to-quantum distillation, where a classical teacher transfers knowledge to a quantum student; and quantum-enhanced classical distillation, where quantum kernels or quantum feature spaces improve the training signal between classical models. Across these categories, the common goal is to reduce the cost and instability of QML training while preserving or improving performance under NISQ constraints. Another clear pattern is that the meaning of "distillation" in QML is broader than in standard deep learning: it can refer to circuit-depth reduction, hardware adaptation, teacher-guided optimization, relational supervision, or parameter-efficient hybrid deployment.

However, several limitations remain open. First, most QML distillation studies are evaluated on relatively small datasets such as MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-like benchmarks, or limited-domain classification tasks, leaving scalability to large vision, language, and scientific datasets underexplored. Second, many results are based on simulation rather than real quantum hardware, so their robustness under shot noise, gate noise, decoherence, and hardware-specific connectivity remains uncertain. Third, current methods often focus on output-level distillation, while intermediate-state distillation, measurement-distribution alignment, circuit-structure transfer, and quantum representation matching remain less developed. Fourth, benchmarking is still uneven: many papers compare against direct quantum training, but fewer compare against strong classical compression baselines such as pruning, quantization, low-rank adaptation, or small classical students trained with standard KD. Finally, theoretical understanding is limited: it is still unclear when a quantum student can faithfully approximate a classical teacher, when quantum relational information provides an advantage over classical kernels, and how distillation affects barren plateaus and optimization landscapes.

In summary, quantum machine learning distillation provides a promising bridge between powerful classical models and compact quantum models. By using teacher-student learning, QML distillation can reduce quantum circuit depth, improve noisy-device performance, reuse classical pretrained models, and potentially enable quantum-enhanced supervision. The field is moving from simple circuit compression and small-scale image classification toward broader hybrid learning frameworks, including LLM distillation, relational distillation, and medical imaging. Future work should focus on real-hardware validation, stronger theoretical guarantees, larger-scale benchmarks, and distillation objectives that exploit uniquely quantum properties rather than merely importing classical KD losses into quantum architectures. If these directions mature, distillation could become one of the main ways QML systems are made practical under NISQ-era constraints.
